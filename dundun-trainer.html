Learning the dunduns, easiest first

How to actually drill it

The trap is practising both hands at once from the start and hoping they separate. They don't — you get two half-learned parts. The bell has to become automatic first, because in performance it is the part that never stops.

  1. Bell alone, until it is boring. Switch the drum lane off above and play only the bell, for minutes at a time, until you can hold a conversation over it.
  2. Sing the drum part over it. Keep playing the bell and only voice the drum strokes. This builds the relationship between the two lines without asking your hands to do anything new.
  3. Add the drum hand on the coincidences only. Every column with an ochre underline is a stroke where both hands move together. Two hands moving as one is not independence, and it is much easier than it looks.
  4. Fill in the rest. Whatever is left is bell-only — so it belongs to the bell hand, which you already made automatic in step 1.
  5. Drop the tempo far lower than feels necessary. Take the slider to 40–55. Coordination is learned at the speed where you can consciously place each stroke; speed arrives on its own.
  6. Practise away from the drums. Bell hand on one knee, drum hand on the other. Most of this is a brain problem, not a hands problem.

Most players give the bell to the weaker hand and the stick to the stronger one, since the drum strokes carry the dynamics — but follow whatever your teacher does, because switching later is genuinely annoying.

How each part sits

Rhythms taught and notated by Michael Agbodo; traditional West-African repertoire, transcribed from his scores — six or seven staves each, on a sixteenth-note grid (Gidamba in twelve-eight). Sticking is positional: every even slot is the right hand, every odd one the left, which is what the sheets print above the djembe staves. Sounds are recorded instruments from Freesound, trimmed and level-matched; the three dunduns come from one session, the djembe strokes from three different drums.
